# Sprint 80 — session one taught a definition session two contradicts

**Date:** 2026-08-30
**Reviewed against:** HEAD `f1532f6`
**Scope:** First Look content only — `foundations/early.ts`, `foundations/upper.ts`,
`foundations/index.ts`, one paragraph of the educator orientation, and a new
content test file. No schema, no action, no component, no new field.

## The finding

First Look session one, *The Guessing Machine*, defined AI for a class that has
never been told what it is:

> AI is a computer program that fills in what usually comes next.

That sentence appeared in the big idea, a learning goal, the board narration, the
wrap-up, the family summary and the family rule — six places, one week apart from
session two, *You Have Met It Already*, which correctly calls a video
recommendation, a face filter and a smart speaker AI. None of those fills in a
word.

For a six year old that is not nuance, it is a contradiction, and the thing that
survives contact with it is **AI = autocomplete**. Every later mission builds on
a category the child does not have: a tool that invents a school swimming team is
not filling in the next word, and neither is an app that asks for a home address.
The curriculum's own orientation says the three assumed ideas are what First Look
exists to install. This installed a fourth one that is wrong.

The example itself is excellent and was not the problem. A next-word predictor is
the right first encounter: a class can watch it work, the pattern is visible, and
"it has never met your cat" lands. The defect is that the example was given the
grammar of a definition.

## The correction

Category first, example second, in that order, everywhere a child or an adult
meets it.

**Session one big idea:**

> An AI program finds patterns in lots and lots of examples and uses them to make
> a guess. This one guesses a word.

**On the board (scene 3):**

> It found patterns in all of them. Now it can guess.
> This one guesses words.

**On the board at the end (scene 7):**

> A guessing machine uses patterns to guess. This one guesses words.

**The wrap-up the class reads out:**

> An AI program finds patterns in lots of examples. Then it guesses.
> This one guesses the word that is likely to come next.
> Likely is not the same as sure.

**The family rule:** *"A guessing machine guesses from patterns it has seen. It
has not met us."*

The three learning goals now separate what the child should generalise (patterns
from many examples produce a guess) from what they met (this one guesses a likely
next word, and likely is not sure).

## Session two now reads as expansion, not correction

Session two's big idea says so explicitly — *"It is inside things you use every
day, and it guesses more than just words"* — and the board list gained the line
that does the work:

> One guesses a word. One guesses what you will like.
> One guesses where your face is.

The callback to session one changed from *"That is the same trick from last
time"* to *"It guesses your word from patterns, like the one last time. Guessing
words is one kind of guess."* The first sentence made the keyboard the archetype;
the second makes it a member.

**And the widened category needed a floor.** Telling children AI is in ordinary
things invites the opposite error, so a new misconception entry answers *"So
everything on a screen is AI"* by contrasting a calculator, which follows a rule
somebody wrote, with a guessing machine, which works from patterns in examples —
ending *"Fast is not the test; guessing from examples is."* The coat-hook
distractor was rewritten to the same standard: *"A hook does not guess anything.
Look for something that makes a guess."*

One line was **deliberately left alone**. The decision-two choice label *"Mat,
because that is what usually comes next"* is a child predicting one specific word
in one specific sentence. That is the correct answer to that question and not a
claim about the category.

## The upper track had the same shape, milder

Grades 3 to 5 said *"An AI tool produces what usually follows"* — categorical in
exactly the same way. Now:

> An AI tool works from patterns in a huge number of examples. A writing tool
> uses them to produce what usually follows, which is why it can be smooth, fast
> and wrong at the same time.

The learning goal, one wrap-up line, the family summary and one family question
were scoped the same way. Remaining instances of the phrase are inside sentences
already about a writing tool and are correct as they stand.

## The adult surfaces

The foundations overview comment and the educator orientation both asserted the
narrow version to teachers, which is worse than asserting it to children —
a teacher repeats it. `cert-1` now reads:

> That an AI program works out patterns from a great many examples and uses them
> to make a guess — producing a likely next word being one kind of guess, and
> matching a face or choosing what plays next being others.

## Known gaps

- **Nothing stops the next categorical sentence.** The tests pin four phrasings
  that exist. A fifth, worded differently, would pass. A content rule that
  demanded the category before any example — the way the word-count and
  sentence-count rules already work mechanically — would be durable; this is a
  denylist.
- **Session three was not re-examined for the same defect.** It was read and
  carries no definition, but the sweep this sprint was driven by the two sessions
  the finding named plus the upper track's echo of it.
- **The core curriculum's 27 missions were not audited for this.** They assume
  the category rather than defining it, which is why they were out of scope — but
  "assume" was also the state First Look was invented to fix.
- **No child has read any of this.** The claim is that a more accurate sentence
  is still age-appropriate, and the evidence for it is a word cap, not a
  classroom.
