# Sprint 11 review — 27 August 2026

**Scope.** Two findings from a human read of Sprint 09 content. Both were
correct, both were content errors rather than code errors, and both are now
fixed and guarded by tests.

**Method.** Ran against the checklist in `../classroom-review.md`. The fixes
are content edits, so the guards had to be written specifically: the automated
suite could check structure and reading level but had nothing to say about
whether a sentence was *true*, or whether an activity was safe to run.

---

## Finding 1 — "The Question With No Answer" taught the right habit with wrong facts

Three inaccuracies, all in student-facing copy, all of the kind a seven year
old would remember as the rule:

- **"February and the mountain have been settled by somebody measuring."**
  Nobody measures February. It follows a calendar rule.
- **"It produces the average opinion."** That is not what the tool does, and it
  gives a child a mental model that will mislead them later.
- **"For an opinion about your own school, it is impossible."** Overclaim. It
  is not impossible for the tool to say something; it has no way to *know*
  unless somebody tells it.

**A fact is now defined as something you can check against evidence, a rule or
a record**, and the mission uses each: the calendar rule for February, a
measurement for the mountain, and nothing at all behind the mud rule. The tool
is described as putting together "words that usually go together when people
write about school rules" — a general answer, assembled from patterns, about a
school it was never told anything about.

The core lesson is unchanged and was always the strong part: a local fact can
inform a value judgement, and the child still has to do the judging. Scene five
still sends them to the caretaker, and their answer still ends up different
from where it started.

Wrap-up, teacher prompts and the family summary all now carry the same
distinction. A misconception was added for "it could work it out if it thought
harder", which is where a bright nine year old goes next.

## Finding 2 — "The Quiz That Kept Asking" contained the hazard it was teaching about

Two problems, and the second was the serious one.

**Full credit for carrying on.** Skipping the two recovery questions and
finishing an unapproved quiz scored `strong` and `demonstrated`. That teaches
that steering around one field makes a service safe, which is exactly the wrong
generalisation. **Stop and tell is now the only full-credit answer**: closing it
and telling Ms. Okafor records `privacy.escalate` demonstrated. Closing it
silently, and skipping the field to carry on, are both `partial` and record
`developing` — safe for that child, and no help to the twenty-two others still
tapping. The sunk-cost option stays a retry.

**An activity that invited real answers.** The extension asked children to
invent two spare-key questions and swap them between tables. Seven to ten year
olds asked to write a security question write the real answer next to it. The
extension now uses **cards the teacher hands out already written**, and states
out loud before starting that nobody writes or says their own answers. It also
tells the teacher what to do if a child volunteers a real pet name anyway.

The family sheet asked "can you think of one?", which invites the same thing at
the kitchen table. It now asks for the *kinds* of question, explicitly not the
answers.

The teacher line "convince somebody else they are your child" had a broken
point of view — the reader is the teacher, so "your child" pointed at the wrong
person. It now reads "convince some other service that they are the student".

## Guards added

Four tests, because neither finding was reachable by the existing suite:

- The recovery-question scene has exactly one `strong` choice, it records
  `privacy.escalate`, and its label mentions telling somebody. The skip-and-
  continue option must be `partial`/`developing`.
- **No mission's extension** may ask students to author security questions or
  swap them between tables, and the quiz extension must carry the
  no-own-answers instruction. The family sheet must say "not your answers".
- The fact-opinion mission must name the calendar rule, must define a fact via
  evidence, rule or record in its wrap-up, and must not say February was
  settled by measuring.
- It must not describe AI output as an "average opinion" or as "impossible",
  and must describe it in terms of patterns.

236 tests, up from 232.

## What this says about the review process

Both findings were content accuracy and content safety. The automated suite has
grown good coverage of structure, reachability, evidence rules, reading level
and banned vocabulary — and none of that could tell that February does not get
measured, or that an activity would have children writing down their own
security answers.

Eighteen missions were authored in a single sprint and shipped on a green
suite. That is the risk the sprint review flagged at the time under "content,
not mechanics", and this is what it looked like when it landed. The checklist
gains an item accordingly.

## Deliberately left

- **The other sixteen Sprint 09 missions have not had a human read.** Two were
  read and two had findings. The rest should be assumed to have some.
- **No general guard against factual error.** The tests above pin these
  specific claims. Nothing stops the next mission asserting something false,
  and nothing could, short of a human reading it.
