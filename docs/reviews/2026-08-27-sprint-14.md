# Sprint 14 review — 27 August 2026

**Scope.** Two findings from a human read of the next two Sprint 09 missions.
Both correct. Eight missions read by hand now, eight with findings.

The second finding is the most serious defect found in this curriculum. It is a
safeguarding problem, not only a verification one.

---

## Finding 1 — "What the Camera Sees" never said which state the camera was in

The mission had an update that "turns on the camera", a preview the child could
see, and a conclusion that **"live video cannot be checked first."** Between
those three things it never established whether anything was being sent.

That matters twice over. Real products separate permission, preview and live
state, and they say so on screen. And the version the mission taught — if you
can see yourself, it is already out — gives a child **no way to tell one from
the other**, so they guess. A vague fear is not a skill.

**All three states are now explicit and observable.**

- **Permission** was granted months earlier, for photographs of the barn. The
  update "does not ask you anything. It already has the camera, so it just
  starts using it." That is both the realistic case and a lesson that pairs with
  the consent-scope fix in The Class Photo.
- **Preview** is labelled: `PREVIEW — ONLY YOU CAN SEE THIS`, with a `GO LIVE`
  button next to it. A new scene turns this into a decision. Theo types "I
  cannot see your room. Have you not gone live yet?" and the full-credit answer
  is *"Nobody has seen it yet. The preview is only on my screen."*
- **Live** is labelled too: `PREVIEW` changes to `LIVE`, and beside it
  `8 PLAYERS CAN SEE THIS NOW`. The little brother walks through the shot, and
  "by the time you spot him on your own screen, eight people have already got
  him on theirs."

**"If I can see it, everybody can see it" is now a retry**, with a coach note
saying why: a child who believes that cannot tell a safe screen from a live one
and will guess. The durable rule is phrased as the finding asked — *once it is
live, each new moment reaches people before you can check it.*

The window clue no longer claims to be "the strongest thing in most rooms"; it
says what a window can reveal. The badge clue says what a school badge reveals.
The classroom activity now uses a photograph of the **empty** room taken before
school, or a prepared image, and says outright: never a live feed, never a fresh
photo with children in it.

## Finding 2 — "The Video of Mr. Ruiz" exonerated a familiar adult

**"Mr. Ruiz would never do that" earned full synthetic-media credit.** The
ending called the person in the video "the one source nobody can fake" and "the
single most reliable check available", and the sign on the library desk read
IF IT IS ABOUT A PERSON, ASK THE PERSON.

Every part of that is wrong, and the way it is wrong is not academic. Familiar
people do act unexpectedly. People deny things they did. A remote channel can be
impersonated. And a mission that teaches seven- to ten-year-olds that an adult
they like is automatically in the clear is teaching something that could stop a
child speaking up about something real.

**Out of character is now a pause, not a verdict.** It is `partial` and records
`developing`: *"It makes you stop, which is what it is for. People do surprise
you, though, and how well you know somebody is not a thing you can check. Take
it as a reason to go looking."* Provenance — *"Where did this come from?"* — is
the strong first move and keeps full credit.

**The subject's account is context.** Mr. Ruiz still watches the clip and still
says it never happened, because that beat is what makes the harm concrete. But
he "walks it straight down to the office, because what a person says about
himself is not the same as somebody checking." In the reflection scene, asking
the person is now the `partial` answer: *"It is one piece though. A message can
be faked, and people are not always right about themselves either."*

**The sign changed**, and so did everything aligned to it. It now reads
IF IT IS ABOUT A PERSON, FIND OUT WHERE IT CAME FROM — which is the strong move,
is something a child can actually do, and is what resolves the story: the office
follows the chat back and finds the app it was made with, and *"that is the bit
that actually settled it."* The wrap-up, the family summary and the family rule
all moved with it.

The wrap-up carries the safe action for a harmful clip: **stop passing it on,
and hand it to a grown-up who can check it properly** — source, witnesses,
context.

The teacher setup now states the safeguarding line explicitly, at length,
because a teacher reading this mission needs to know why it is built this way:
never let it teach that a familiar adult is automatically in the clear, and a
child who learns that people they like could not have done a thing has learned
something that may stop them speaking up later. Two misconceptions carry the
sentences a class will actually say — *"He said he did not do it, so that is
that"* and *"You can tell he would never do that."*

### Caught in the browser

The teacher preview showed "SAFE CHOICE — Records developing" on the
out-of-character option: the tone had been left `strong` while the evidence was
demoted. The preview renders tone and evidence from different fields, so nothing
failed. Aligned to `partial`, and the guard now asserts both.

## Guards added

Nine tests, plus four phrases added to the brittle-absolutes ban list, refused
across the whole curriculum: `live video cannot be checked`, `nobody can fake`,
`single most reliable`, `the strongest thing in most rooms`.

257 tests, up from 249.

## What this says about the review process

Eight read, eight with findings.

This sprint is the first where a defect was a **safeguarding** issue rather than
an accuracy or pedagogy one, and it is worth being blunt about how it got there:
the mission was written to make a fake feel like something done to a person you
care about, which is good instructional design, and the warmth of that framing
carried an exoneration rule in with it. Nothing in the structural validation,
the evidence rules, the reading level or the vocabulary checks could see it.
Neither could sprint 12's "true as a general rule" item, because the sentence
*is* about a general rule — it is just a rule that should not be taught.

The checklist gains an item for it.
