# Sprint 19 review — 27 August 2026

**Scope.** Two findings on original ownership missions. Both correct. Six of the
original nine have now had a proper read, and six had findings.

The first is the first *structural* content defect found here: a shared scene
that cannot be true on every path leading into it. Not a wording problem — a
graph problem.

---

## Finding 1 — "The Homework That Did Itself" awarded honesty for a false report

The mission is about the difference between a hint and an answer. Its reporting
scene had one strong disclosure:

> *"Sprocket gave me a hint on number two and I did the rest."*
> **Clear and true.** — `own.honesty` demonstrated

But scene four offered *"Keep number two's answer and redo the other five
yourself"*, also at full credit. On that path the tool supplied the answer and
the child kept it. There was no hint. The mission awarded mastery in honesty for
describing an answer as a hint — which is exactly the downplaying it exists to
teach against, in its own last five minutes.

Working through the graph, the problem was wider than the report stated. Three
opening choices lead into scene four, and one of them — pressing enter without
asking for anything — means no hint was ever given at all. Of the four
combinations reaching the disclosure scene, **one** made its full-credit
sentence true.

### What changed

Scene four now branches. Two choices clear the page and route to the shared
report; keeping Sprocket's answer routes to **its own reporting scene**.

- **Clearing the page** is full credit twice over: do all six yourself, or clear
  it and have a proper go at number two before asking anything — which is the
  mission's own "try first" learning goal, previously unreachable.
- **Keeping the answer** is now `partial`/`developing`. It is allowed and it is
  less of the practice: *"Five out of six is real work. Just remember that an
  answer you kept is not a hint you used, and those two get said differently."*

On the shared report, neither strong option says "hint" — they say *"I asked it
for help with number two and it did the whole page, so I cleared it"* and
*"Sprocket filled the whole thing in without being asked."* Both are true on
every path that arrives there, including the one where nothing was asked for
beyond "I am stuck", because the child did type exactly that.

On the kept-answer report, the accurate sentence earns mastery and **the old
strong line is now the partial**: *"Nearly, and hint is the wrong word. It did
not point you at anything. It handed you the answer and you kept it... Calling
it a hint makes the help sound smaller than it was."*

The worked-versus-posed ambiguity is fixed too. The example Sprocket offers now
"leaves it blank underneath", so the child works it, and the report is honest.

## Finding 2 — "The Spelling Test Surprise" put spelling in the hand

The story is explicit: the child copied each generated sentence **in their own
handwriting** and wrote each word five times. Then the mission explained the
failure as *"the tablet did the spelling, so my hand never learned it"* —
`demonstrated` — with feedback saying the letters "went from the app to the page
without ever going through you", a plan that "the words go in when your hand
writes them", and an ending where the pencil moves because the child "put it
there".

The story contradicts its own explanation. The hand did write them, all week.

What was missing was **recall**: producing the letter sequence without the
answer in view. Copying a visible word is recognition; Friday asks for
retrieval. And the version the mission taught is not merely imprecise — it says
spelling is stored in handwriting, which is false and which lands worst on
children who type, dictate, use assistive technology, or have dysgraphia. A
class taking that lesson home has been told that the way some of them work
cannot teach them anything.

### What changed

The strong answer is now *"I copied every word. I never once wrote one without
looking"*: **"Copying a word sitting in front of you and finding one in your head
are two different jobs. All week the answer was on the screen, so you only ever
did the first one."**

The plan for next week is deliberately modality-free. Cover the word and have a
go from memory, then check — and the second full-credit option says it outright:
*"Out loud, typed, on a whiteboard, with letter tiles, whatever you and your
grown-up have agreed. How the letters come out does not matter much. Having them
out of sight while you find them matters a lot."*

"Messy is the evidence" is gone. **"Every crossing-out is a word you tried
before you knew"** — *"Some people's tries come out neat and some come out a
mess, and that part is not the point."*

The family sheet had the same defect in its one instruction: *"the practice has
to happen in their handwriting."* It now says to cover the word, "however they
give you the letters — written, typed, said out loud, spelled on their fingers".

A misconception was added for the sentence a class will say out loud: *"So you
have to handwrite them or it does not count."*

## Guards added

Six tests, plus four phrases banned across the curriculum: `my hand never
learned it`, `when your hand writes them`, `in their handwriting`,
`messy is the evidence`.

The path guard is the one worth keeping. It asserts that the clearing choices
share a `next` and the keeping choice does not, that no strong option in the
shared report contains the word "hint", and that calling a kept answer a hint is
`partial` wherever it appears.

**315 tests, up from 309.**

## What this says about the review process

Every guard written here so far reads one scene at a time. This finding needed
two scenes held together — an action in scene four and a sentence in scene five
— and no amount of care on either scene alone would have surfaced it. The
mission is internally consistent everywhere you look; it is only wrong when you
walk it.

`validateMission` already walks the graph for reachability and evidence rules.
It cannot check that a disclosure is *true* on the paths that reach it, and
nothing can, short of somebody tracing the branches. The checklist gains that as
an item, phrased as a thing to do rather than a thing to notice.

The second finding is the fourth inclusion defect in five sprints — after the
familiar-adult exoneration, the peer-profiling, and the practice-makes-recall
promise. All four were written warmly and none was visible from the sentence
alone; each needed the question *who does this fail?*
