# Sprint 20 review — 27 August 2026

**Scope.** Two findings on original missions. Both correct. Eight of the
original nine have now had a proper read, and eight had findings.

The second is the most serious defect found in this project. It is not that a
mission taught something inaccurate; it is that a mission's scoring penalised
exactly the children it exists to protect.

---

## Finding 1 — "The Very Sure Answer" swapped one authority shortcut for two

The mission's whole job is to break the habit of believing something because of
how it sounds. It then handed the class two replacements.

**"Calculators do not."** The retry for *"computers do not make mistakes"* was
headlined *"Calculators do not. This is different."* That tells a seven-year-old
that one kind of computer is infallible, which is the same confidence error in
a different wrapper. A calculator executes exactly the arithmetic it is given —
which is a different claim from answering the question correctly. Wrong sum,
wrong units, wrong assumption, and it returns a flawless wrong answer.

**A named author.** The full-credit fact-check was *"look it up in a book about
fish and see who wrote it"*, and the feedback read: *"The book has an author,
and the author is a person who studied fish."* It does not follow. A name on a
cover says somebody wrote it. It says nothing about whether they study the
subject, or whether this particular claim was checked.

### What changed

Execution is now separated from verification. The retry reads: **"A calculator
does exactly the sum you type"** — *"Which is not the same as being right. Type
the wrong sum and it hands you a perfect answer to the wrong question. AskMe is
different again: it puts likely words together, and nothing here shows it
checked anything."*

Mastery in the fact-check now comes from finding a source **answerable for the
claim**: the school's science site, *"because it says where the page came from"*
— it names the aquarium that keeps the fish and the study they did, and you can
go back and find the same page tomorrow. Mr. Ruiz stays full credit, because he
shows you where he looked.

The book is now `partial`: **"Halfway. Now find out who they are."** *"A name on
a cover says somebody wrote it, not that they study fish or that they checked
this bit. Turn to the back: this one says where the author works and lists where
the facts came from. That is the part that counts."*

The guide's calculator-versus-language-tool model was the same error in teacher
form — *"only one is checking anything"* — and now says neither of them checks
whether the child asked the right thing.

## Finding 2 — "The Question at Bedtime" scored a child's family

The reflection scene asked **"How many trusted grown-ups is a good number to
have?"** *At least three, so somebody is always around* earned `demonstrated`.
One genuinely trusted adult earned `developing`. The wrap-up required three, the
extension had every child write three on a card, and the family sheet asked for
three.

Two things are wrong with that, and the second is serious.

**Three names guarantee nothing.** Availability is not a function of list
length, and the mission promised it was.

**More importantly, it measured family size and called it a skill.** Three is
comfortable for a child with a large, safe family and impossible for a child in
foster care, in an unstable home, or in a household where an adult is the
problem. Those are the children this mission exists for, and the mission scored
them lowest and then asked them to write the shortfall on a card at their desk.

And the family rule read: *"Nothing on a screen gets to be a secret from the
grown-ups in this house."* That is unsafe wherever a caregiver is the source of
harm.

### What changed

**The count is gone entirely.** The scene now asks the question that actually
matters and that nobody teaches: *"What do you do if you tell somebody, and
nothing happens?"*

Full credit goes to the two answers that are actionable:

- **"Tell somebody else. Keep going until somebody does something."** —
  *"Grown-ups get busy, or miss it, or get it wrong. If the first person does
  not help, it is not finished and it is not your fault."*
- **"Tell somebody at school. School is there every weekday."** — *"Your
  teacher, the office, whoever your school says to go to. It does not depend on
  anything at home."*

Telling one trusted person and leaving it there is `partial` — the right first
move, and *"one person is plenty to start with"*. The mission never says an
adult is safe because of their role; it says trusted, and the child decides.

The wall sentence replaces the roster: **IF YOU TELL SOMEBODY AND NOTHING
HAPPENS, TELL SOMEBODY ELSE.**

The extension is now a two-line card — somebody I could tell, somebody at school
I could tell — where **one line filled in is fine**, the school's own help route
goes on the board first so nobody starts from nothing, nothing is collected or
read out, and the teacher is told what to do about a blank card: quietly, and
separately. The guide says out loud why the number was dropped, and names foster
care and unsafe households as the reason.

The family rule is now **"If something on a screen wants to be a secret, we tell
a grown-up we trust."**

## Guards added

Six tests. The quota one is a **sweep across every mission**, because this is
the kind of thing that comes back: no choice recording `demonstrated` may use
quota wording about trusted adults, and no scene prompt may ask a child how many
they have.

Writing it took three attempts, and the two failures are worth recording. The
first banned the literal string `at least three`, which is legitimate in The
Science Fair Fact, where it is about counting *sources*. The second used a
pattern loose enough that `twenty-three people` matched `three people`, and that
`putting two adults in touch with each other` matched as a quota. A sweep has to
be narrow enough to mean one thing.

**321 tests, up from 315.**

## What this says about the review process

Nineteen sprints of review have been about whether the content is *true*. This
one is about who the scoring punishes, and it is the fifth inclusion defect in
six sprints. The pattern across all five is the same: warmly written, plausible
as a sentence, and wrong as soon as you ask which child it fails.

This one is worse than the other four, because the evidence model gave it teeth.
A child in foster care did not merely read something unhelpful — they answered
honestly, were recorded at `developing`, and that flowed to a teacher dashboard
as a competency gap. The mission converted a child's home circumstances into
what looks like a skill deficit and reported it upward.

Nothing in the reporting layer could have caught that; the pipeline was working
exactly as designed. It had to be caught in the content, by asking who cannot
give the full-credit answer. That question now sits in the checklist as an item
of its own rather than as a clause inside another one.

## Deliberately left

- **One of the original nine still has no systematic read.** Eight read, eight
  with findings.
- **Nothing checks what a wrong answer costs a child.** The evidence model is
  well tested for integrity — no forced awards, no coached mastery — and not at
  all for whether the honest answer is available to everybody.
- Teacher-facing copy still promises timelines it cannot support.
