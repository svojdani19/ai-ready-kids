## Finding 1 — "The Study Group" ranked private facts against each other

The reflection scene asked **"Which of these is the most useful thing for a
stranger to learn?"** and then answered it by putting other private facts down:
faces were "not much use" without a location, books "lead nowhere", routines
won "every time".

Read by a seven year old, that is a hierarchy, and a hierarchy gives permission:
*everything except my bus route is fine, then.* It directly contradicts The
Class Photo and What the Camera Sees, both of which exist to protect exactly the
things this scene was dismissing.

**The question is now a test, not a league table:** *"Which one of these tells
somebody exactly where to find you, and when?"* A child can apply that to a
question they have never seen. "Most useful to a stranger" cannot be applied to
anything; it can only be memorised as a ranking.

- **Routines** win because they carry a **time** as well as a place — "your
  school, your route and your finishing time add up to a spot and an hour to be
  there." Especially sensitive, for a stated reason, not universally most
  valuable.
- **Faces** are now "yours to keep, and it does not say where" — private,
  staying private, and with a forward pointer to the fact that pictures often
  carry a place inside them.
- **Books** are fine to share *here*, because "nobody can wait for you at a
  book" — a reason, not a dismissal.

The wrap-up carries the contextual rule and adds a line the mission previously
left to inference: *your photos, your face and the rest of your details are
still yours to keep.* The teacher setup warns explicitly that a child who leaves
thinking a photo is fine as long as they hold back the bus has learned the
opposite of two other missions, and a misconception entry — "So photos are fine,
as long as I do not say my bus" — puts that in the teacher's hands before it
settles.

## Finding 2 — "The Helper and the Teacher" taught brittle verification

Three separate conflations, and together they taught a child to verify badly.

**Three examples were treated as proof.** *"Both give the same answers, every
time... which means neither one is wrong."* Three sums are three sums. It now
reads *"Three sums are good evidence, not proof, so tomorrow you can ask Ms.
Okafor to show you why counting up works"* — and the classroom scene where she
draws the number line now explicitly closes the gap: she shows why counting up
*has to* work, "every time and not just for your three sums."

**A single mismatch condemned a method.** *"A different answer means one of them
is wrong, and then you go and find out which."* For this age group that is
backwards — when a child's two methods disagree, the overwhelmingly likely cause
is an arithmetic slip. It now reads *"If one day they do not match, check your
steps first. Usually somebody has slipped, not the method,"* and the wrap-up
carries it as a rule.

**Certainty was called meaningless.** *"Certainty is the one thing that tells
you nothing at all."* Insufficient, not meaningless — a sure voice is often
right, which is exactly why it is persuasive. Now: *"Certainty on its own does
not settle it. A sure voice can be right and a sure voice can be wrong."*

The teacher setup said **"only one of them can show its working"**, which is
false about AI tools and will age badly in front of any teacher who has seen one
produce a page of steps. It now says only one of them showed any working *here*,
and states the limit: a tool can produce working, and when it does that working
is another claim to check rather than proof. A new misconception — "If the app
showed its working, that would prove it" — gives the teacher the answer.

## Found while verifying — mission counts hardcoded at 9

The discussion guide footer read **"Mission 19 of 9"**. The teacher dashboard
had the same stale constant in "N of 9 missions assigned". Both are left over
from when the curriculum was nine missions; sprint 09 tripled it and neither was
caught, because no test asserts on footer copy and nobody had read the bottom of
a guide since. Both now derive from `MISSIONS.length`.

## Guards added

Six tests, plus four new entries in the brittle-absolutes ban list
(`most useful thing a stranger`, `most useful thing for a stranger`,
`is not much use`, `lead nowhere`, `tells you nothing at all`), which are now
banned across every mission's student copy rather than only in these two.

- The study group's reflection prompt must ask where and when, must not say
  "most useful", and the face option must still be described as private.
- Its wrap-up must state the school-plus-route-plus-time rule and must say the
  rest is still the child's to keep.
- Matching examples must be called evidence and not proof, and the copy must
  send the child to the teacher for the reason.
- A mismatch must route to "check your steps" before the method.
- Certainty must be insufficient, not meaningless.
- The guide must not claim a tool cannot show working, and must describe an AI
  explanation as a claim to check.

242 tests, up from 236.

## What this says about the review process

Sprint 11's finding was that eighteen missions shipped in one sprint on a green
suite, and that two of the two read so far had defects. This sprint read two
more. Both had defects. Four for four.

That is now a rate, not an anecdote. The remaining fourteen Sprint 09 missions
should be assumed to contain findings of the same kind — accurate-sounding
shortcuts and rankings that a child will over-generalise — and the only way to
find them is to read them.

The checklist gains one item, because both of this sprint's findings share a
shape the existing items did not name: copy that is true of the case in front of
the child but false as the general rule they will take from it.

- **The eight legacy forced-award scenes**, and their untraced interaction with

- No general guard against factual error exists or can. The bans above are
  literal phrases.
